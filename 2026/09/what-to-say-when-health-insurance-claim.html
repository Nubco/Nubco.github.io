What to Say When a Health Insurance Claim Is Denied

By Daniel Han · September 2, 2026

A denial letter reads like a final answer. It is not. Under federal rules, a denial opens a two-stage process with deadlines that bind the insurer as tightly as they bind you, and the second stage puts the decision in front of someone who does not work for the company that denied you. Most people never get there, because the first letter does not explain that the path exists. Here is what the process is, what to write, and the dates that matter.

This article is for general informational purposes only and is not legal, medical, or insurance advice. Appeal rights and deadlines vary by plan type — self-funded employer plans, marketplace plans, Medicare, and Medicaid follow different rules — and some protections do not apply to grandfathered plans. Read your own plan documents and denial notice, and contact your state insurance department or a licensed professional about your specific situation.

Read the Denial for One Thing First

Before writing anything, find the stated reason. Denials fall into rough categories, and the category determines your argument. Administrative denials — wrong code, missing prior authorization, out-of-network processing, coordination-of-benefits confusion — are often fixed by correcting a record rather than by arguing medicine. Medical-necessity denials are a judgment call you can contest with clinical evidence. Denials that call a treatment experimental or investigational are a third category with its own route. Answering the wrong category is the most common reason a strong appeal goes nowhere.

The 180-Day Window

You have 180 days — six months — from receiving notice that your claim was denied to file an internal appeal. That is a generous window compared with most consumer deadlines, and it is also the one people miss, because a denial arrives, gets set aside, and the six months pass. Diary the date the day the letter arrives. If you are close to the deadline and still gathering records, file the appeal anyway with what you have and supplement it; a filed appeal preserves the timeline, an unfiled one does not.

What to Actually Put in the Internal Appeal

Keep it short and structured. Identify the claim by member ID, claim number, date of service, and provider. State the reason given for the denial, quoting the letter. State what you are asking for, in one sentence. Then attach the evidence that meets the stated reason: for medical necessity, a letter from the treating physician explaining why the service was needed and what the alternatives were; for an administrative denial, the corrected code or the authorization number. Ask in writing for the plan documents and the specific criteria used to make the decision. Close with the date you expect a response, using the deadlines below.

The Deadlines Your Insurer Is Held To

The insurer does not get to take as long as it likes. An internal appeal must be completed within 30 days if it concerns a service you have not yet received, and within 60 days if it concerns a service you have already received. For urgent situations, a final decision must come as quickly as your medical condition requires and at least within four business days of the request; that decision can be delivered verbally but must be followed by written notice within 48 hours. Knowing these numbers changes the tone of a follow-up call from a request into a reminder.

If the Internal Appeal Fails: External Review

This is the stage most people never reach. After a final internal denial you can request an external review, in which an independent reviewer outside your insurance company decides. You must file a written request within four months of the date you receive the final determination. A standard external review is decided no later than 45 days after the request is received; an expedited review is decided no later than 72 hours, or sooner if the urgency requires. The part that matters most: your insurer is required by law to accept the external reviewer's decision.

Which Denials Qualify for External Review

Not everything goes to external review. It covers denials involving medical judgment where you or your provider disagree with the plan, determinations that a treatment is experimental or investigational, and cancellations of coverage based on claims that you gave false or incomplete information on your application. A purely administrative dispute — a billing code, a clerical error — is generally resolved at the internal stage instead. Reading your final internal denial for which category it falls into tells you whether the external route is open.

Expedited Review When Waiting Is the Problem

When a delay would seriously jeopardize your health or your ability to regain function, you do not have to run the standard clock. You can request an expedited internal appeal, and in urgent cases you can request an external review at the same time rather than waiting for the internal process to finish. Say the word urgent explicitly in the request and have the treating physician document why the timing matters. The compressed deadlines above only apply if the request is made and documented as urgent.

Keep a Record as You Go

Every appeal is a paper trail argument. Keep the original denial, dates of every call with the name of who you spoke to, copies of everything you send, and proof of when you sent it. If your plan comes through an employer, the benefits administrator can escalate internally and is often faster than the member line. If the insurer misses its own deadlines, your state insurance department is the place to raise it — that is what the deadlines are for.
