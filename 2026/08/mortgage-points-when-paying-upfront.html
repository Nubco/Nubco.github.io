Mortgage Points: When Paying Upfront Actually Pays Off

By Daniel Han · August 28, 2026

Somewhere in the loan estimate is a line offering to lower your interest rate if you pay more at closing. That's a discount point, and whether it's a good deal comes down to arithmetic plus one prediction you can't make with certainty: how long you'll keep the loan. The arithmetic is straightforward and most people never run it. The prediction is where the real risk lives, because a break-even that arrives in year seven is worthless if you refinance in year three.

What a Point Actually Buys

One discount point costs one percent of the loan amount and reduces your interest rate by a set amount, often somewhere around a quarter of a percentage point, though the exact reduction varies by lender, loan type, and market conditions. On a $400,000 loan, a point costs $4,000 at closing. Points are usually available in fractions, so you might buy half a point or one and a half. The lender is selling future interest income for cash today; you're buying a lower payment for the life of the loan with money you hand over now.

The Break-Even Calculation

Divide the cost of the points by the monthly payment reduction to get the number of months required to recover the cost. If a point costs $4,000 and lowers the payment by $60, the break-even is roughly 67 months, or about five and a half years. Keep the loan past that and the points come out ahead; sell or refinance before it and you lost money. This is the calculation lenders will run for you if asked, and it is the single most useful number in the decision.

Why Break-Even Isn't the Whole Answer

The simple calculation ignores what the same money could have done elsewhere. Four thousand dollars invested, or used to avoid mortgage insurance, or kept as an emergency reserve, has value the break-even math doesn't capture. It also ignores taxes: discount points on a primary residence may be deductible, though only if you itemize, which most filers no longer do. A more honest version of the question is whether the guaranteed return implied by the rate reduction beats what you'd otherwise do with the cash, adjusted for the risk that you don't keep the loan.

When Points Usually Pay Off

Points favor borrowers who are confident they'll hold the loan a long time: a house you intend to stay in, a fixed-rate loan you're unlikely to refinance because the rate is already low relative to history, and cash available beyond your reserves and down payment. They also help borrowers who need a lower payment to qualify, since reducing the rate reduces the debt-to-income ratio. And a seller or builder credit that pays for points, sometimes structured as a temporary buydown, changes the calculation entirely, because you're not spending your own money.

When They Usually Don't

Points rarely pay off when you might move within a few years, when rates are high enough that refinancing later looks probable, when paying for them would drain the reserves you need after closing, or when the same cash would let you avoid private mortgage insurance by reaching a higher down payment tier. Adjustable-rate loans complicate the math further, since the rate resets and the discount may apply only to the initial period. If you can't say with reasonable confidence that you'll hold the loan past break-even, that's usually your answer.

Discount Points vs. Origination Points

These sound alike and are not the same thing. Discount points buy down your rate. Origination points are a fee the lender charges to process the loan and buy you nothing in return. Both appear as a percentage of the loan amount, which is why they get confused. On a loan estimate, discount points appear as a credit against the interest rate while origination charges sit with other lender fees. When comparing offers, separate the two, because a loan with low origination and high discount points is a different product from the reverse.

Negative Points and Lender Credits

The trade runs both directions. Instead of paying to lower your rate, you can accept a higher rate in exchange for a lender credit toward closing costs. That makes sense when cash at closing is the binding constraint, or when you expect to refinance or move soon enough that the higher rate never costs you much. The break-even logic is the same, reversed: you're borrowing against future interest to solve a present cash problem. Ask your lender to quote the same loan at several rate-and-point combinations, then compare them on total cost over the period you actually expect to hold the loan.
