How to Dispute an Error on Your Credit Report

By Daniel Han · August 26, 2026

Credit report errors are more common than most people expect, and they can quietly cost you money through higher interest rates or a denied application. Worse, most people never find out, because the error only surfaces when a lender declines them and they go looking for the reason. The dispute process is free, follows a defined procedure, and carries legal deadlines the companies involved are required to meet. Here's how to work through it properly.

This article is for general informational purposes only and is not legal or financial advice. Dispute procedures and your rights under the Fair Credit Reporting Act can be complex. For guidance specific to your situation, consult the CFPB's resources at consumerfinance.gov or speak with a qualified professional.

Step 1: Get Your Reports and Find the Error

You're entitled to free copies of your credit reports from the three nationwide credit reporting companies, Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion, through AnnualCreditReport.com. Pull all three, because they don't always contain the same information and an error may appear on only one. Look for accounts you don't recognize, payments marked late that you made on time, balances that are wrong, accounts still showing open that you closed, and duplicate listings of the same debt.

Step 2: Dispute With the Credit Reporting Company

File your dispute in writing with each bureau reporting the error. Your letter should include your full name, address, and contact information, identify the specific item you're disputing including the account number, clearly explain why you believe it's incorrect, and include copies of any supporting documents. Send copies, never originals. The bureaus can also be reached by phone: Equifax at 1-866-349-5191, Experian at 1-888-397-3742, and TransUnion at 1-800-916-8800, though a written record is generally more useful if the dispute drags on.

Step 3: Dispute With the Furnisher Too

This is the step people skip, and it matters. Credit reporting companies don't originate the information; they collect it from furnishers, meaning the bank, lender, landlord, or credit card company that reported it. If you only dispute with the bureau, the furnisher may keep supplying the same incorrect data. Send a separate written dispute directly to the furnisher, and consider using certified mail so you have proof of what you sent and when it arrived.

What Happens Next, and How Long It Takes

Once a dispute is filed, the credit reporting company investigates and reports back on the outcome. Furnishers are generally required to investigate and respond within 30 days of receiving the dispute. If the investigation confirms the information was inaccurate, it must be corrected or removed. You can also request that the corrected report be sent to anyone who received your report recently, which matters if the error affected a specific application.

If the Dispute Doesn't Go Your Way

A dispute being closed doesn't have to be the end. You can add a brief statement of dispute to your credit file explaining your side, which appears alongside the disputed item. You can also refile with additional documentation if you obtain evidence you didn't have the first time. If a company appears to be ignoring the process, you can submit a complaint to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, which forwards complaints to the company and tracks the response.

Which Errors Are Worth Prioritizing

Not every inaccuracy carries the same weight. Payment history is the single largest factor in most credit scoring models, so a payment incorrectly marked 30 or more days late is among the most damaging items to leave uncorrected. Accounts that aren't yours at all deserve immediate attention, since they can indicate identity theft rather than a clerical mistake. Incorrect balances or credit limits matter too, because they distort your credit utilization, the second-largest scoring factor. By contrast, a misspelled former address or an outdated employer is usually harmless and rarely worth the effort unless you're correcting a broader identity mix-up.

Keep Records of Everything

Save a copy of every letter you send, note the date it was mailed, and keep delivery confirmations. Log phone calls with the date, who you spoke with, and what was said. If a dispute stretches over months or you need to escalate it, this paper trail is what makes the difference between a frustrating back-and-forth and a resolvable case.
