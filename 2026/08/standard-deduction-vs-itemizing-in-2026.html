Standard Deduction vs. Itemizing in 2026: Which One Applies to You

By Daniel Han · August 27, 2026

Every federal return involves one early choice: take the standard deduction, or itemize. Since a 2017 law roughly doubled the standard deduction, the large majority of filers now take it, and itemizing has become the exception rather than the default. That shift means a lot of advice still circulating online assumes an itemizing landscape that no longer describes most households. Here's how to tell which side of that line you're actually on for 2026, and what to do if you're near it.

The 2026 Standard Deduction Amounts

For tax year 2026, the IRS set the standard deduction at $16,100 for single filers and for married taxpayers filing separately, $32,200 for married couples filing jointly and for surviving spouses, and $24,150 for heads of household. These are the figures your itemized deductions would need to exceed before itemizing produces any benefit at all. Taxpayers who are 65 or older, or blind, may qualify for an additional amount on top of the base figure.

What Actually Counts as an Itemized Deduction

The main categories are state and local taxes, which include income or sales tax plus property tax and are subject to a cap; mortgage interest on a qualifying loan; charitable contributions to eligible organizations; and medical expenses, but only the portion exceeding a percentage threshold of your adjusted gross income. Casualty losses in federally declared disaster areas can also qualify. Notably absent are the everyday expenses people assume count, such as commuting costs, most work expenses for employees, and personal credit card interest.

The Arithmetic Is the Whole Decision

Add up your qualifying itemized deductions for the year. If the total exceeds your standard deduction, itemize; if it doesn't, take the standard deduction. There is no partial credit and no strategic advantage to itemizing a smaller amount. For a married couple filing jointly in 2026, itemized deductions would need to clear $32,200 before the first dollar of benefit appears, which is a high bar without a substantial mortgage or large charitable giving.

Who Still Ends Up Itemizing

The typical itemizer has one large deduction doing most of the work. A recent mortgage on an expensive home generates significant interest in its early years, when payments are mostly interest rather than principal. Substantial charitable giving, particularly of appreciated assets, can push a return over the threshold. A year with major uninsured medical expenses is another common case. Homeowners in high-tax states may also find property and income taxes contribute meaningfully, though the cap on that category limits how far it goes.

The Bunching Strategy

If your itemized total lands just below the standard deduction each year, you get no benefit from those deductions at all. One response is to concentrate discretionary deductible expenses into alternating years: make two years of charitable contributions in a single calendar year, itemize that year, then take the standard deduction the next. Donor-advised funds are often used for this, letting you take the deduction when you contribute while distributing to charities over time. The tactic only helps if your near-threshold total is driven by expenses whose timing you actually control.

Above-the-Line Deductions Don't Require Itemizing

A separate set of deductions reduces your taxable income regardless of which route you take. Traditional IRA contributions, if you're eligible, health savings account contributions, and student loan interest within the applicable limits all fall here. This matters because people sometimes conclude that taking the standard deduction means no other deductions are available to them, and that's not how it works. These reduce your income before the standard-versus-itemized choice is even reached.

Keep the Records Either Way

You can't know which side you land on until you total the year, and reconstructing charitable receipts or medical bills in April is considerably harder than filing them as they arrive. Keeping a simple folder through the year costs almost nothing and preserves the option. It also matters if you're audited: the standard deduction requires no substantiation, but any itemized deduction you claim does.
