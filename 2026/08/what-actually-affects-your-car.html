What Actually Affects Your Car Insurance Rate (Ranked by Impact)

By Daniel Han · August 26, 2026

Two drivers with identical cars on the same street can pay wildly different premiums. Insurers price policies using a long list of variables, but a handful of them do most of the work. Here's what actually moves your rate, roughly in order of impact.

This article is for general informational purposes only and is not personalized insurance advice. The figures cited are national averages and industry estimates; your own rate depends on your insurer, state, and individual circumstances. 1. Your Driving Record

Nothing raises a premium faster than an at-fault accident. Industry rate surveys (not government data) put a single property damage accident at roughly a 57 percent increase in premium, and an accident involving bodily injury pushes that closer to 63 percent. Two property damage accidents can more than double a rate, with increases around 123 percent. Most accidents and violations affect your rate for three to five years depending on the state and insurer, so a clean record is the single most valuable thing you can protect.

2. Your Age

Age is one of the strongest predictors of claim risk, and pricing reflects that sharply. The Insurance Information Institute lists age as a standard rating factor; the dollar figures below come from commercial rate surveys rather than a regulator. Teen drivers aged 16 to 19 pay an average of roughly $5,613 per year, while drivers aged 20 to 24 average around $2,976. Rates settle considerably for adults aged 25 to 60, averaging near $1,903, and drop slightly again for drivers aged 65 to 75 at about $1,864. This is why adding a teen driver to a family policy causes such a dramatic jump.

3. Your Credit History

In most states, insurers use a credit-based insurance score as a rating factor, and the effect is large. The Federal Trade Commission, in its report to Congress on credit-based insurance scores, found that these scores do predict both the number of claims a driver files and what those claims cost. Rate surveys put the practical gap at roughly 117 percent more for drivers with poor credit than for drivers with good credit on otherwise identical coverage. Four states prohibit or heavily restrict this practice: California, Hawaii, Massachusetts, and Michigan. California is the clearest case: under Proposition 103, every rating plan must use three mandatory factors - driver safety record, annual mileage, and years of driving experience - and credit is not among the optional factors the state permits. If you live elsewhere, improving your credit over time is one of the more powerful levers you have on your premium.

4. Where You Live

Rates are set at the ZIP code level, not just the state level, because claim frequency varies enormously by neighborhood. Areas with higher accident rates, vehicle theft, vandalism, or severe weather exposure cost more to insure. The effect can be substantial: rate surveys have associated a move from San Francisco to San Diego with roughly a 30 percent decrease in premium, without changing anything else about the driver or vehicle.

5. What You Drive

Vehicle choice matters less than people assume, but it still matters. Insurers look at repair costs, parts availability, theft rates for that model, horsepower, and crash test performance. High-performance and luxury vehicles cost more to insure because parts and labor are expensive and claims tend to be larger. A common family sedan or SUV with strong safety ratings typically sits at the lower end of the range.

What This Means Practically

The factors you can influence most are your driving record and your credit, and both work slowly, over years rather than months. The factors you can influence quickly are coverage choices, deductibles, and which insurer you buy from. That's why comparison shopping matters: because each company weights these variables differently, the same driver can get meaningfully different quotes from different insurers on the same day.
