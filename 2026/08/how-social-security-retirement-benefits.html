How Social Security Retirement Benefits Are Actually Calculated

By Daniel Han · August 27, 2026

Social Security is the single largest source of retirement income for many Americans, and the amount you receive is far less fixed than people assume. Two decisions — when you claim, and how long you worked — move the number substantially. Here's what actually drives it.

This article is for general informational purposes only and is not personalized financial advice. Social Security rules are detailed and depend on your birth year, earnings record, and family situation. Verify your specifics at ssa.gov or with a qualified professional before making a claiming decision.

Your Earnings Record Comes First

Your benefit is built from your own history of earnings on which Social Security taxes were paid. The calculation looks at your highest-earning years, adjusts them for changes in average wages over time, and produces an average monthly figure. That average is then run through a formula to arrive at the benefit you'd receive at your full retirement age. Two practical implications follow: years with little or no covered earnings pull the average down, and additional working years later in life can replace earlier low-earning years in the calculation.

Full Retirement Age Is Not 65

This is the most common misunderstanding, and it has real financial consequences. Full retirement age depends on your birth year. For people born between 1943 and 1954, it is 66. For anyone born in 1960 or later, it is 67, with the ages in between phasing gradually upward. Sixty-five is the Medicare eligibility age, not the Social Security full retirement age, and conflating the two leads people to claim earlier than they intended.

Claiming Early Cuts the Benefit Permanently

You can claim as early as 62, but the reduction is significant and does not reverse when you later reach full retirement age. For someone born in 1960 or later, a benefit that would have been $1,000 at full retirement age is reduced to $700 if claimed at 62 — a 30 percent cut. For those born between 1943 and 1954, the reduction at 62 is 25 percent. The reduced amount is what you receive for the rest of your life, subject to annual cost-of-living adjustments.

Delaying Past Full Retirement Age Increases It

The reverse also holds. For anyone born in 1943 or later, delaying past full retirement age earns delayed retirement credits worth 8 percent per year. Those credits stop accruing at 70, so there is no benefit to waiting beyond that. The spread is substantial: for someone with a full retirement age of 67, claiming at 62 versus 70 produces a monthly difference of well over 70 percent of the base amount, for the same earnings record.

So When Should You Claim?

The tradeoff is between more years of smaller checks and fewer years of larger ones. Claiming early makes more sense if you need the income now, if you have health conditions that meaningfully shorten your expected lifespan, or if claiming lets you avoid drawing down invested assets during a market downturn. Delaying makes more sense if you can cover expenses from other sources, expect to live into your eighties or beyond, or want to maximize the survivor benefit for a lower-earning spouse, since that benefit is based on the higher earner's amount.

Check Your Earnings Record Now, Not at 62

Your benefit is calculated from what the Social Security Administration has on file, and errors do occur, particularly around name changes, employers that reported incorrectly, or self-employment income. Create an account at ssa.gov and review your posted earnings history against your own records. Correcting an error is far easier close to when it happened than decades later, and the estimate the site provides also gives you a concrete number to plan around instead of a guess.

One Thing to Verify Before Deciding

Several rules can affect your specific situation in ways a general article cannot cover: working while collecting before full retirement age can temporarily withhold part of your benefit, benefits may be partly taxable depending on your total income, and spousal and survivor rules have their own timing considerations. Before locking in a claiming decision, confirm the details against ssa.gov or with a professional who can look at your actual record.
