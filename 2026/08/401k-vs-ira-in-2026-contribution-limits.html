401(k) vs. IRA in 2026: Contribution Limits and Which to Prioritize First

By Daniel Han · August 26, 2026

If your employer offers a 401(k) and you're also eligible for an IRA, you may wonder where to put your savings first. The two accounts work differently, have different 2026 contribution limits, and are taxed differently depending on the type you choose. Here's a practical, numbers-first breakdown to help you decide.

2026 Contribution Limits at a Glance

For 2026, the IRS raised the employee contribution limit for 401(k) plans to $24,500, up from $23,500 in 2025. The IRA contribution limit (covering both Traditional and Roth IRAs combined) increased to $7,500, up from $7,000. If you're 50 or older, you can contribute an extra $8,000 to a 401(k) as a catch-up contribution, bringing your total to $32,500. Workers aged 60 to 63 get an even higher catch-up limit of $11,250 under recent rule changes. For IRAs, the 50-and-older catch-up is $1,100, for a total of $8,600.

Employer Match: Free Money You Shouldn't Skip

If your employer matches 401(k) contributions, that match is typically the first place your savings should go, regardless of which account you eventually prioritize for the rest of your savings. A common match structure is 50% or 100% of your contribution up to a percentage of your salary. Leaving an employer match on the table is effectively giving up guaranteed, immediate returns that no investment can reliably match.

Traditional vs. Roth: Why the Type Matters More Than the Account

Both 401(k)s and IRAs come in Traditional and Roth versions. Traditional contributions reduce your taxable income now, but withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Roth contributions are made with after-tax dollars, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Whether a Traditional or Roth account makes more sense depends heavily on whether you expect your tax rate to be higher or lower in retirement than it is today. Note that Roth IRA eligibility phases out at higher incomes (for 2026, roughly $153,000 to $168,000 for single filers, and $242,000 to $252,000 for married couples filing jointly), while Roth 401(k)s have no income limit.

A Practical Order for Funding Both

A commonly used approach, though not one-size-fits-all, is: first, contribute enough to your 401(k) to get the full employer match. Second, consider maxing out an IRA, since IRAs often offer a wider range of low-cost investment choices than an employer's 401(k) menu. Third, if you still have money to save after that, return to your 401(k) and increase contributions toward the $24,500 limit (or higher with catch-up contributions). This order isn't a rule everyone should follow exactly — someone with a 401(k) that has notably low-cost, high-quality fund options might reasonably prioritize it further, and your personal tax situation may change what makes sense.

A Note on Traditional IRA Tax Deductibility

If you or your spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan, your ability to deduct Traditional IRA contributions phases out at certain income levels. For 2026, that range is roughly $81,000 to $91,000 for single filers covered by a workplace plan, and $129,000 to $149,000 for married couples filing jointly. If neither spouse is covered by a workplace plan, contributions are generally fully deductible regardless of income. Even if you can't deduct a Traditional IRA contribution, you can still contribute to a Roth IRA (if under the income limit) or make a non-deductible Traditional contribution.
