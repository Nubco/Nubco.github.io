Tax Credits Most People Miss

By Daniel Han · August 27, 2026

Deductions get most of the attention at tax time, but credits are worth considerably more per dollar. A credit reduces what you owe directly, one dollar of credit for one dollar of tax. A deduction only reduces the income that tax is calculated on. Despite that, several valuable credits go unclaimed every year, usually because people don't know the rules apply to them.

This article is for general informational purposes only and is not tax advice. Credit amounts, income thresholds, and eligibility rules change from year to year and depend on your circumstances. Confirm current figures at IRS.gov or consult a qualified tax professional before filing.

Refundable vs. Nonrefundable Is the Key Distinction

A nonrefundable credit can reduce your tax bill to zero but no further; any leftover credit is lost. A refundable credit can take you past zero, producing a refund even if you owed no tax to begin with. This matters enormously for lower-income filers, who may assume there's no point filing if they owe nothing. With refundable credits, filing is exactly how you collect money that's owed to you. Some credits are partially refundable, meaning a capped portion can come back as a refund.

The Earned Income Tax Credit

The EITC is refundable and aimed at low- to moderate-income workers and families. The amount varies with your income, family size, and filing status, and it can be substantial for households with children. It's among the most commonly missed credits, in part because eligibility changes as circumstances change: a year with reduced income, a new child, or a change in filing status can make you eligible when you weren't before. If your situation shifted at all, it's worth rechecking rather than assuming last year's answer still holds.

The Saver's Credit

Formally the Retirement Savings Contributions Credit, this is a nonrefundable credit for contributing to an IRA or an employer retirement plan, worth up to $1,000 for individuals and $2,000 for married couples filing jointly. It's routinely overlooked because people don't realize a credit exists on top of any deduction the contribution already provided. If you contributed to a retirement account and your income falls within the eligible range, this is close to free money you have to claim to receive.

Child-Related Credits

The Child Tax Credit is partially refundable and was recently worth up to $2,200 per qualifying child, with up to $1,700 of that refundable. Separately, the Child and Dependent Care Credit is nonrefundable and covers a portion of what you paid for care that allowed you to work or look for work. The two are distinct and are not alternatives, so a household paying for daycare while working may qualify for both. Both carry specific age, residency, and relationship requirements worth reading rather than assuming.

Education Credits

The American Opportunity Tax Credit is partially refundable and worth up to $2,500 for undergraduate education expenses, with up to $1,000 of that refundable. The Lifetime Learning Credit covers a broader range of coursework, including graduate study and courses taken to improve job skills, but is nonrefundable. You generally cannot claim both for the same student in the same year, so the calculation is which one produces the larger benefit for your situation.

Why These Get Missed

Three patterns account for most of it. People assume they earn too much or too little to qualify without checking the actual thresholds. People whose situations changed mid-year apply last year's conclusion to this year. And people who owe no tax skip filing entirely, forfeiting refundable credits that would have paid out. Free filing options exist for many taxpayers, and the arithmetic strongly favors filing even when nothing is owed.

How to Check Systematically

Rather than trying to recall which credits exist, work from the IRS's own list of credits for individuals and read the eligibility conditions for each, particularly for any year in which your income, household, employment, or education status changed. Most tax software walks through these questions, but only answers them correctly if you provide complete information about dependents, care expenses, and retirement contributions. Amended returns are also possible within a limited window if you discover a credit you missed in a prior year.
