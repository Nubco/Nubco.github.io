Debt Avalanche vs. Snowball: Which One Should You Actually Use?

By Daniel Han · August 26, 2026

Two strategies dominate the conversation about paying off multiple debts, and they give opposite advice about which balance to attack first. One is mathematically superior. The other has a better track record of people actually finishing. Which should win depends on something specific about you.

How the Avalanche Method Works

With the debt avalanche, you make minimum payments on everything, then direct every extra dollar toward the debt carrying the highest interest rate. When that one is cleared, you roll the entire payment into the next-highest-rate debt, and so on. Because you're always attacking the debt that grows fastest, this approach mathematically minimizes total interest paid and, for the same monthly budget, gets you debt-free soonest.

How the Snowball Method Works

The debt snowball orders debts by balance rather than interest rate. You make minimums on everything and throw extra money at the smallest balance first, regardless of its rate. Clear it, then roll that payment into the next-smallest. You will pay more interest overall than with the avalanche, sometimes noticeably more, but you eliminate individual accounts faster in the early months.

Why the Mathematically Worse Method Often Wins

Debt payoff fails far more often from abandonment than from bad arithmetic. The snowball's advantage is behavioral: closing an account entirely delivers a visible, concrete win, and research on consumer behavior consistently finds that early, tangible progress helps people sustain effort on long tasks. If your highest-rate debt is also your largest, the avalanche can mean a year or more of payments with nothing crossed off the list, which is exactly when people quit.

How Much Does the Difference Actually Cost?

Before assuming you should optimize, calculate the gap for your own numbers. If your debts carry similar interest rates, the two methods finish within weeks of each other and the extra interest from choosing snowball is trivial. If one debt is at 24 percent and the rest are at 6 percent, the avalanche's advantage is substantial and worth some discomfort. The honest answer only comes from running both against your actual balances and rates.

A Hybrid That Often Fits Best

These aren't the only two options. A practical compromise: clear one or two of your smallest balances first for the psychological win and to simplify the number of accounts you're tracking, then switch to strict avalanche ordering for the rest. You capture most of the motivational benefit while giving up only a small amount of the interest savings.

What Matters More Than Either Method

Both strategies assume a fixed monthly amount going toward debt, and the size of that amount affects your payoff date far more than the ordering does. Before agonizing over sequence, look at whether you can increase the total, and whether refinancing or a balance transfer could cut the rates themselves. Lowering a 24 percent rate does more than any reordering of payments will.
