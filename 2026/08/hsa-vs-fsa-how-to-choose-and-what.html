HSA vs. FSA: How to Choose and What Happens to the Money

By Daniel Han · August 28, 2026

Health savings accounts and flexible spending accounts both let you pay medical costs with pre-tax dollars, which is where the similarity ends. They have different eligibility rules, different limits, and completely different answers to the question that matters most: what happens to money you don't spend by December 31. For most people the choice isn't really a choice, because eligibility settles it. Here is how to work out which one you can use, and what to do with it once you have it.

Eligibility Decides This Before You Do

An HSA requires enrollment in a qualifying high-deductible health plan and no other disqualifying coverage. For 2026, a qualifying plan must have a deductible of at least $1,700 for self-only coverage or $3,400 for family coverage, with out-of-pocket maximums no higher than $8,500 and $17,000 respectively. You also cannot be enrolled in Medicare or claimed as someone else's dependent. A health FSA, by contrast, is offered through an employer and does not depend on your plan type, but it's only available if your employer offers one. If you're self-employed, an FSA is off the table.

The 2026 Numbers

HSA contributions for 2026 are capped at $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, with an additional $1,000 catch-up contribution allowed once you turn 55. Employer contributions count toward those limits. The 2026 health FSA salary reduction limit is $3,400. Both accounts reduce taxable income, and HSA contributions made through payroll also avoid Social Security and Medicare tax, which is a real advantage over contributing directly and deducting later. Limits are per person for FSAs, so two working spouses may each be able to fund one.

What Happens to Money You Don't Spend

This is the sharpest difference. HSA balances roll over indefinitely with no deadline and no forfeiture. FSA funds are generally use-it-or-lose-it: unspent money reverts to the employer at year end unless the plan offers relief. Employers may allow one of two options, not both. A carryover permits up to $680 of unused 2026 funds to move into the next plan year, or a grace period gives up to two and a half extra months to incur expenses. Many plans offer neither, so check yours before deciding how much to elect.

Portability: The Practical Difference

An HSA belongs to you. Change jobs, change plans, or retire and the account and its balance come with you. An FSA is tied to your employer, and leaving usually ends access to unspent funds, though COBRA continuation is sometimes available. There is one FSA quirk worth knowing: your full annual election is available on day one of the plan year, even though you fund it over twelve months, and if you leave mid-year having spent more than you contributed, the employer generally absorbs the difference. HSAs fund as you go, so the balance is only what you've deposited.

The HSA's Third Tax Advantage

Both accounts give you a deduction going in and tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses. The HSA adds a third layer: the balance can be invested, and growth is tax-free as long as withdrawals go to qualified expenses. That makes an HSA function as a long-term account rather than a spending account, and some people deliberately pay current medical costs from cash, keep the receipts, and let the balance compound for decades. After age 65 you can withdraw for any purpose, paying ordinary income tax but no penalty, which makes it behave like a traditional retirement account for non-medical use.

When You Can Have Both

A general-purpose health FSA disqualifies you from contributing to an HSA, including in many cases a spouse's FSA that could reimburse your expenses. The exception is a limited-purpose FSA, which covers only dental and vision costs and preserves HSA eligibility. Some employers offer one alongside an HSA specifically so employees can fund predictable dental and vision spending separately. If you're weighing both during open enrollment, ask benefits whether the FSA on offer is general-purpose or limited-purpose, because the answer changes what else you can do.

How to Choose

If you're eligible for an HSA and can absorb the higher deductible from savings, the HSA is usually the stronger account: it rolls over, it invests, it's portable, and the tax treatment is better. If you're not eligible, or you know a high deductible would push you to skip care you need, an FSA is a straightforward way to make predictable costs pre-tax. Either way, size the election from actual spending history rather than a round number, and for an FSA err low, since the downside of under-electing is smaller than forfeiting a balance.
