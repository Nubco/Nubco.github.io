How Your Credit Score Is Actually Calculated

By Daniel Han · August 26, 2026

Credit score advice tends to be a pile of tips with no sense of priority, which leaves people optimizing things that barely matter. FICO publishes the weighting of the five factors that build the score. Once you know the percentages, it becomes obvious where to focus.

This article is for general informational purposes only and is not personalized financial advice. Scoring models vary between FICO versions and VantageScore, and individual results depend on your full credit file. Check your own reports at AnnualCreditReport.com and consult a licensed professional for guidance specific to your situation.

Payment History — 35%

This is the largest single factor by a wide margin. It reflects whether you've paid past credit accounts on time. Late payments, accounts sent to collections, and bankruptcies all land here, and a payment reported 30 or more days late can affect your score for years. Practically, this means automating at least the minimum payment on every account is the highest-value habit available to you. Nothing else on this list comes close.

Amounts Owed — 30%

This measures how much of your available credit you're currently using, often discussed as credit utilization. Using a large share of your available credit reads as risk, even if you pay in full each month, because the balance reported to the bureaus is usually your statement balance rather than what remains after payment. Paying down balances before the statement closing date, rather than after, is what actually lowers the reported figure.

Length of Credit History — 15%

This looks at the age of your oldest account, the age of your newest account, and the average age across all accounts. It's the factor you have the least direct control over, since it only improves with time. The one lever it gives you: closing an old credit card can shorten your average account age, so keeping a long-held no-fee card open, even with minimal use, generally helps.

Credit Mix — 10%

Scores give modest weight to the variety of credit you manage, spanning credit cards, retail accounts, installment loans, and mortgages. Demonstrating you can handle both revolving credit and installment debt reads slightly better than only one type. This is not a reason to take out a loan you don't need. At 10 percent, the cost of borrowing unnecessarily far outweighs any scoring benefit.

New Credit — 10%

Opening several accounts in a short window is treated as elevated risk, and each application typically generates a hard inquiry. The practical implication matters most around large purchases: avoid opening new accounts in the months before applying for a mortgage or auto loan, since a cluster of recent inquiries and new accounts can pull your score down exactly when it counts most.

What This Means in Practice

Payment history and amounts owed together account for 65 percent of the score. If you never miss a payment and keep reported balances low relative to your limits, you've addressed the substantial majority of what the model measures. The remaining three factors reward patience and restraint more than any specific action, which is why most quick-fix credit tactics deliver far less than advertised.
