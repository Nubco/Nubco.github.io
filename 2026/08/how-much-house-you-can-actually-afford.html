How Much House You Can Actually Afford

By Daniel Han · August 28, 2026

A pre-approval letter answers one question: how much a lender is willing to risk on you. That is a different question from how much you can comfortably carry for the next thirty years, and the gap between the two is where a lot of financial stress starts. Lenders underwrite to default risk, not to whether you'll still be able to save for retirement or absorb a new roof. Here is what goes into their number, what they leave out, and how to build your own figure before you start looking.

Consult a qualified mortgage professional and review current program guidelines before making a home purchase decision.

Pre-Approval Is a Ceiling, Not a Target

Lenders approve based on documented income, existing debt, credit history, and the down payment you bring. They do not know your childcare costs, how much you contribute to retirement, whether your income is variable, or how much you spend on things you're not willing to give up. A pre-approval is genuinely useful as a ceiling and as proof to sellers that you're a real buyer. Treating it as a target is how buyers end up technically able to make the payment and unable to do anything else with their money.

The Three Numbers Lenders Evaluate

The first is debt-to-income ratio, which comes in two forms: the front-end ratio compares your projected housing payment to gross monthly income, and the back-end ratio compares total monthly debt payments, housing included, to gross income. Conventional underwriting has historically clustered around a back-end ratio in the mid-thirties to mid-forties, with flexibility for strong compensating factors. The second is credit score, which shifts your interest rate and therefore your payment. The third is the down payment, which affects both loan size and whether you'll owe mortgage insurance.

PITI, Not Just Principal and Interest

The number a mortgage calculator produces from price, rate, and term is only part of the payment. Lenders evaluate PITI: principal, interest, taxes, and insurance, plus any mortgage insurance and homeowners association dues. Property taxes and premiums are usually collected monthly into escrow, and in high-tax jurisdictions they can add several hundred dollars to a payment. They also rise over time independently of your fixed-rate loan, which is why a payment that felt comfortable at closing can drift upward. Always compare houses on full PITI, not on principal and interest.

The Costs That Never Show Up in the Payment

Maintenance is the big one. A common planning heuristic is one to two percent of the home's value per year, averaged across quiet years and the year the water heater and the roof fail together. Then there are the costs of the transition itself: closing costs, moving, immediate repairs, window coverings, and the furniture a larger space seems to demand. Utilities typically rise with square footage. A longer commute has a real monthly cost. None of this appears in a lender's ratio, and all of it comes from the same paycheck.

A Conservative Test You Can Run

Start from take-home pay rather than gross, since that is the money that actually arrives. Subtract the retirement contribution you intend to keep making, not the one you'd cut. Subtract childcare, insurance premiums, debt payments, and a realistic maintenance reserve. What remains is what a full PITI payment can occupy without crowding out everything else. Then stress-test it: could you still make the payment if one income paused for six months, or if property taxes and insurance rose meaningfully? If the answer is no, the price is above your real ceiling regardless of the approval.

The Down Payment Changes More Than the Price

A larger down payment reduces the loan, which reduces the payment and the total interest, and crossing twenty percent equity on a conventional loan removes private mortgage insurance. But draining savings to reach that threshold trades one risk for another: a house with no emergency fund behind it is fragile, because home ownership generates unpredictable expenses on its own schedule. Many buyers are better served putting less down, keeping cash reserves intact, and removing mortgage insurance later through appreciation or extra principal payments.

If the Number Comes Out Lower Than You Hoped

The three levers are price, down payment, and time. Paying down a car loan or credit card can move your back-end ratio enough to change what you qualify for, and it improves your actual cash flow either way. Improving your credit score before applying can lower the rate, which quietly raises what a given payment can buy. Waiting a year to build reserves is not a failure; it's the difference between owning a home and being owned by one. A smaller house you can maintain beats a larger one you can only barely make payments on.
