What to Do With Your 401(k) When You Change Jobs

By Daniel Han · August 26, 2026

Leaving a job means making a decision about the retirement account you built there, and it's easy to postpone it or take the option that looks simplest. The four choices differ enormously in what they cost you. Here's what each one actually does.

Option 1: Roll It Into an IRA

Moving the balance into an individual retirement account is a direct, tax-free transfer if done as a rollover. The money keeps growing tax-deferred, and an IRA typically offers a far wider range of investment choices than an employer plan's limited fund menu. This also lets you consolidate multiple old 401(k)s into one account, which makes them easier to track and rebalance.

Option 2: Roll It Into Your New Employer's Plan

If your new employer's plan accepts incoming rollovers, and most do, you can move the old balance in. This keeps everything in one place and preserves tax-deferred growth. It can be the better choice if your new plan has unusually low-cost institutional funds, or if you value the stronger creditor protection that employer plans carry in some states.

Option 3: Leave It Where It Is

Many plans let you leave your balance with your former employer if it's above a minimum threshold. The money continues growing tax-deferred, but you can't make new contributions, and you're stuck with that plan's fund lineup and fees. The practical risk is that forgotten accounts tend to go unmanaged for years, drifting out of alignment with the rest of your portfolio.

Option 4: Cash It Out (Usually the Costly One)

Taking the money as cash triggers ordinary income tax plus, if you're under 59 and a half, an additional 10 percent early withdrawal penalty. The numbers are stark. On a $50,000 balance, roughly $12,000 might go to federal tax, $3,500 to state tax, and $5,000 to the early withdrawal penalty, leaving about $29,500. You'd lose more than 40 percent of the balance, plus every dollar of growth it would have produced over the following decades.

One Detail That Catches People Out

The early withdrawal penalty is generally not withheld at the time of distribution. Your plan may withhold for income tax, but the 10 percent penalty typically comes due when you file your tax return. That means a cash-out can leave you owing money the following April that you've already spent.

Direct vs. Indirect Rollovers

When rolling over, request a direct rollover, where the money moves institution to institution and never passes through your hands. In an indirect rollover, the plan sends you a check, typically withholding 20 percent for taxes, and you have 60 days to deposit the full original amount, including the withheld portion, into the new account. Miss the deadline or fail to replace the withheld portion, and the shortfall is treated as a taxable distribution.
