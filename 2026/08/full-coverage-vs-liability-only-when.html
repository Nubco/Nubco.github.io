Full Coverage vs. Liability Only: When It's Safe to Drop Full Coverage

By Daniel Han · August 27, 2026

Once a car is paid off and a few years old, the question comes up every renewal: is full coverage still worth what it costs? There is no age at which the answer flips automatically. What matters is the relationship between what the car is worth, what the coverage costs, and what you could absorb without it. Here is how to work that out for your own situation.

What 'Full Coverage' Actually Means

Full coverage isn't a product an insurer sells; it's shorthand for carrying liability plus two optional coverages. Collision pays for damage to your own car from a crash, regardless of fault. Comprehensive pays for non-collision losses like theft, hail, fire, vandalism, or hitting a deer. Liability, which every state requires in some amount, pays for other people's injuries and property when you're at fault. Dropping full coverage means keeping liability and removing collision and comprehensive. It never means driving uninsured.

When You Don't Get to Choose

If you're financing or leasing, the lender or leasing company almost certainly requires both collision and comprehensive for the life of the loan, because the car is their collateral. Dropping it can put you in breach of the loan agreement, and lenders typically respond by force-placing coverage that costs considerably more than what you'd buy yourself. This decision only becomes yours once the car is owned outright.

The Test Worth Running

Pull your declarations page and find the annual premium attributable to collision and comprehensive specifically, not your total bill. Then look up your car's actual cash value, which is what the insurer would pay if it were totaled, not what you feel it's worth or what a dealer would list it for. Now subtract your deductible from that value. That difference is the most the coverage can ever pay you. If your annual cost for collision and comprehensive is a large fraction of that maximum payout, the coverage is buying you very little.

A Worked Example

Suppose collision and comprehensive cost $600 a year, your car's actual cash value is $3,000, and your deductible is $1,000. The most a total loss claim could pay is $2,000. You are spending $600 annually to protect a $2,000 maximum recovery, meaning roughly three and a half years of premiums equals the entire benefit. On the other hand, if the same $600 protects a $14,000 car, the arithmetic looks completely different, and dropping the coverage would be a serious gamble.

The Question the Math Doesn't Answer

Even when the numbers favor dropping full coverage, one question decides it: if the car were destroyed tomorrow, could you replace it without borrowing or derailing your finances? If losing the car would mean losing your way to work and you have no cushion to replace it, keeping the coverage buys stability that the expected-value calculation doesn't capture. Insurance is for losses you can't absorb, and that threshold is personal rather than mathematical.

What Not to Cut Along With It

If you do drop collision and comprehensive, resist the temptation to reduce liability limits at the same time to save more. Those protect against the losses that can genuinely follow you, since a serious at-fault accident can generate claims far beyond what a minimum policy covers. Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage also deserves to stay, particularly once you've dropped collision, because it becomes your only protection when someone without adequate insurance hits you. The money saved by removing full coverage is often better spent raising those limits than pocketed.

Revisit It Every Renewal

This isn't a one-time decision. Your car's value falls every year while the premium for collision and comprehensive typically doesn't fall as fast, so the ratio steadily worsens. A car that clearly justified full coverage two years ago may not now. Running the same calculation at each renewal takes about ten minutes and is one of the few insurance decisions where the answer genuinely changes over time.
