Deductible, Copay, Coinsurance, Out-of-Pocket Max: Health Insurance Terms Explained

By Daniel Han · August 26, 2026

Health plan documents describe your costs using four terms that sound similar and are easy to confuse. They interact in a specific order over the course of a year, and understanding that order is what lets you compare two plans honestly instead of just looking at the monthly premium.

This article is for general informational purposes only and is not personalized insurance or medical advice. Plan rules vary significantly, including whether copays apply before the deductible. Review your plan's summary of benefits and coverage, or contact your insurer, for details specific to your policy.

Premium: What You Pay to Have Coverage

Your premium is the fixed amount you pay each month to keep the plan active, whether or not you use any care. It's the only one of these costs you pay regardless of what happens. Importantly, premiums do not count toward your deductible or your out-of-pocket maximum, which is why a low-premium plan isn't automatically the cheaper plan overall.

Deductible: What You Pay Before the Plan Starts Sharing

The deductible is the amount you pay for covered services before your insurance begins paying its share. If your deductible is $2,000, you're generally responsible for the full negotiated cost of covered care until you've paid $2,000 in a plan year. One common exception: many plans cover certain preventive services, and sometimes a set number of primary care visits, before the deductible is met.

Copay: A Flat Fee Per Visit

A copay is a fixed dollar amount for a specific service, such as $30 for a primary care visit or $15 for a generic prescription. You pay it at the time of service. Whether copays apply before or after you meet your deductible depends on the plan, and this varies enough that it's worth checking your specific summary of benefits rather than assuming.

Coinsurance: Your Percentage Share After the Deductible

Once you've met your deductible, you typically don't pay nothing. You pay a percentage of covered costs, called coinsurance, while the plan pays the rest. An 80/20 plan means insurance covers 80 percent and you pay 20 percent. On a $5,000 procedure after your deductible is met, that's $1,000 out of your pocket.

Out-of-Pocket Maximum: The Ceiling That Protects You

This is the most important number for worst-case planning. Once your combined deductible, copays, and coinsurance reach this cap in a plan year, the plan pays 100 percent of covered, in-network care for the rest of the year. Premiums don't count toward it, and neither do out-of-network charges or services the plan doesn't cover. This figure, not the deductible, is what caps your exposure in a genuinely bad year.

Putting It Together When Comparing Plans

Compare plans by adding twelve months of premiums to the out-of-pocket maximum. That total is your realistic worst case for the year. Then estimate a typical year based on care you actually expect. A high-deductible plan with a low premium often wins if you rarely need care, while a higher-premium plan with a lower deductible and out-of-pocket maximum can cost less if you have ongoing prescriptions or expect a procedure. Also confirm your doctors and medications are in network, since out-of-network care generally does not count toward these limits.
