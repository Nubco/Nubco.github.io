How Much Life Insurance Do You Actually Need?

By Daniel Han · August 26, 2026

Most people either guess at a coverage amount or take whatever their employer offers and assume it's enough. Neither approach is grounded in what your family would actually need. Here are three methods for arriving at a real number, from the quickest rule of thumb to the one worth spending twenty minutes on.

Method 1: Ten Times Your Income

The simplest approach multiplies your annual income by ten. Someone earning $75,000 a year would target roughly $750,000 in coverage. This is fast and better than guessing, but it ignores your specific debts, savings, existing coverage, and how many years your family would actually need support. Treat it as a starting point rather than an answer.

Method 2: Ten Times Income Plus Education Costs

A refinement adds roughly $100,000 per child for future education costs on top of the ten-times-income figure. A parent earning $75,000 with two children would target about $950,000. This captures one large expense the basic rule misses, but it still doesn't account for your mortgage balance, other debts, or the savings you've already built.

Method 3: The DIME Formula

DIME stands for Debt, Income, Mortgage, and Education, and it's the method most worth your time because it works from your actual obligations. Add up: your debts excluding the mortgage, plus final expenses; your annual income multiplied by the number of years your family would need support; your remaining mortgage balance; and estimated education costs for your children. Then subtract savings and any coverage you already have. The remainder is your gap.

A Worked Example

Consider a 45-year-old parent earning $75,000 who wants to cover 15 years of income. Income need: $1,125,000. Remaining mortgage: $100,000. Other debts: $25,000. Estimated education costs: $120,000. Final expenses: $20,000. That totals $1,390,000. After subtracting existing savings and an employer policy, the remaining need lands near $1,200,000. Notice how far this sits from the $750,000 the simple rule would have suggested.

Don't Forget What You Already Have

Before shopping, tally coverage you already hold. Many employers provide group life insurance equal to one or two times salary at no cost, and some people have older individual policies they've forgotten about. Subtract these from your calculated need. Also consider that employer coverage usually ends when you leave the job, so it shouldn't be the foundation of your plan if your family depends on it.

How Long Should the Term Be?

If you're buying term life insurance, match the term length to the period your family is financially dependent on you. Common anchors are the year your youngest child finishes college, the year your mortgage is paid off, or your expected retirement date, whichever is furthest out. Buying a longer term than you need raises the premium; buying a shorter one risks coverage expiring while it's still needed.
