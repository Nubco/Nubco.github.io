High-Yield Savings vs. CDs vs. Money Market: Where to Park Your Cash

By Daniel Han · August 26, 2026

Once you've built up cash beyond your checking account, the question becomes where it should actually sit. These three options all pay meaningfully more than a standard savings account, but they trade off differently between access and rate certainty. Here's how to match them to what the money is for.

This article is for general informational purposes only and is not personalized financial advice. Rates, minimums, and terms vary by institution and change over time. Verify current terms directly with the bank or brokerage and confirm insurance coverage at FDIC.gov before depositing funds.

High-Yield Savings: Access First

A high-yield savings account lets you withdraw whenever you need to, though some banks limit the number of transactions per month. The rate is variable, meaning it moves when broader interest rates move, so what you earn today isn't guaranteed next year. Minimum balance requirements are typically low or nonexistent. Deposits at an FDIC-insured bank are covered up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category.

CDs: Rate Certainty in Exchange for Commitment

A certificate of deposit locks your money for a set term, commonly ranging from a few months to five years, in exchange for a fixed rate that holds for the entire term. That fixed rate is the real appeal: if rates fall, you keep earning what you locked in. The tradeoff is access. Withdrawing before the term ends usually triggers an early withdrawal penalty, often several months of interest. Minimums vary by institution, frequently in the $500 to $1,000 range. CDs at insured banks carry the same $250,000 FDIC coverage.

Money Market: Know Which Product You're Buying

This is where people get tripped up, because two different products share the name. A money market deposit account is a bank product, FDIC-insured up to $250,000, often with check-writing or debit access and a higher minimum balance. A money market fund is an investment product held at a brokerage. It is not FDIC-insured; brokerage accounts carry SIPC protection up to $500,000, which covers broker failure rather than investment losses. Both offer good liquidity, but the protection behind them is not the same thing, so check which one you're actually opening.

Matching the Account to the Goal

For an emergency fund, access matters more than squeezing out the last fraction of a percent, so a high-yield savings account is usually the right home. For money with a known date attached, like a down payment 18 months out or a tax bill due next spring, a CD matching that timeline lets you lock a rate you can count on. For a larger cash balance you want to keep liquid while earning something, a money market account sits between the two.

A Note on CD Laddering

If you like the fixed rates CDs offer but dislike committing everything for years, laddering splits the difference. Instead of putting $20,000 into one five-year CD, you might open five $4,000 CDs maturing one year apart. Each year one matures, giving you access to a portion without penalty, and you can reinvest at whatever rates prevail then. It reduces both the pain of needing money early and the risk of locking everything in right before rates rise.

Watch the Fine Print, Not Just the Headline Rate

Advertised rates often carry conditions. Some accounts pay the headline rate only up to a balance cap, with anything above it earning far less. Others require a minimum balance, direct deposit, or a linked checking account, and quietly drop the rate if you fall out of compliance. Promotional rates may also expire after an introductory period. Before moving money, confirm the rate applies to your full balance and what you'd need to do to keep it.
