How Much Car Insurance Do You Actually Need?

By Daniel Han · August 26, 2026

Car insurance shopping often starts and ends with one question: what's the cheapest policy that's legal to drive with? But state minimum coverage is usually far less than what you actually need, and a serious accident can leave you personally on the hook for the difference. Here's how the main coverage types work and what limits are worth paying for.

The Coverage Types, Explained Simply

Liability coverage is split into two parts: bodily injury liability, which pays for the other driver's medical bills, lost wages, and pain and suffering when you're at fault, and property damage liability, which pays to repair or replace what you hit. Collision coverage pays for damage to your own car from a crash, regardless of fault. Comprehensive coverage handles non-collision events like theft, weather damage, vandalism, or hitting an animal. Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage protects you if the other driver has no insurance or not enough of it.

Why State Minimums Usually Aren't Enough

Every state sets a minimum liability requirement, often written as three numbers like 25/50/25, meaning $25,000 per person for bodily injury, $50,000 total per accident, and $25,000 for property damage. These minimums were set years ago and haven't kept pace with medical and repair costs. Consider a real example: an accident generates a $105,000 claim. With a 25/50/25 policy, insurance covers $50,000, and the at-fault driver is personally responsible for the remaining $55,000, a bill that can lead to wage garnishment or a lawsuit.

What Coverage Level Actually Makes Sense

A common rule of thumb ties recommended liability limits to your assets, since your assets are what's at risk in a lawsuit beyond what insurance covers. For someone with under $50,000 in assets, 50/100/50 is a reasonable baseline. For most people with $50,000 to $500,000 in assets, 100/300/100 is the most commonly recommended level. If you have $500,000 to $5 million in assets, consider 250/500/250 along with an umbrella policy that adds protection above your auto and home coverage.

Collision and Comprehensive: When They're Worth It

If you're financing or leasing your car, your lender will typically require both collision and comprehensive coverage. If you own your car outright, it comes down to what you could afford to replace out of pocket. As a rough guide, if your car's market value is low relative to the annual premium for collision and comprehensive combined, it may make more sense to drop that coverage and self-insure the vehicle's value instead.

Don't Skip Uninsured Motorist Coverage

It's tempting to treat uninsured motorist coverage as optional, but it protects you in situations liability insurance can't touch: being hit by a driver who has no insurance or not enough of it. Because a notable share of drivers nationally are uninsured, and rates vary significantly by state, this coverage is generally worth keeping even on a tighter budget, often at a fairly low added cost relative to the protection it provides.
