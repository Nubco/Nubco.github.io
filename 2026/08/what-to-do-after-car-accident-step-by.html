What to Do After a Car Accident: A Step-by-Step Guide

By Daniel Han · August 27, 2026

The minutes after a collision are exactly when clear thinking is hardest and when the decisions that shape your claim get made. Knowing the sequence in advance is worth more than any single piece of advice, because it removes the need to improvise while your hands are still shaking. Here is what to do, in order.

This article is for general informational purposes only and is not legal, medical, or insurance advice. Reporting requirements and claim rules vary by state and by policy. Contact your insurer, and consult a licensed professional, for guidance specific to your situation.

Safety Comes Before Everything Else

Check yourself and your passengers for injuries before anything else, and call emergency services if anyone is hurt. If the vehicles are drivable and you're in a live traffic lane, move them to the shoulder or a nearby lot; a second collision at the scene is a real risk. Turn on hazard lights. If the cars can't be moved safely, stay belted inside with hazards on rather than standing in traffic. Nothing about documentation or insurance matters more than not being hit again.

When to Call the Police

Call in any accident involving injuries, significant property damage, a driver who appears impaired, or a driver who refuses to share information. Many states also require a report above a damage threshold. Even when it isn't strictly required, a police report creates an independent record of the scene, which becomes valuable if the other driver's account changes later. If officers don't come to the scene, ask whether you can file a report at the station or online, and get the report number.

What to Document Before You Leave

Photograph both vehicles from several angles, including wide shots that show their positions relative to each other and the road, plus close-ups of every point of damage. Capture the other car's license plate, the intersection or mile marker, traffic signals and signage, skid marks, debris, and the weather and lighting conditions. Exchange names, phone numbers, insurance companies, policy numbers, and driver's license numbers. If anyone stopped and saw what happened, ask for their name and number, because witnesses become nearly impossible to find afterward.

What Not to Say at the Scene

Apologizing is a reflex, and at an accident scene it can be read as accepting fault. Stick to exchanging information and describing what happened factually if asked by police. Avoid speculating about causes, guessing at speeds, or assuring the other driver that you'll cover the damage privately. It's also worth knowing that people frequently feel fine immediately after a crash and develop symptoms hours or days later, so declining medical attention outright and stating you're uninjured can complicate a later injury claim.

Reporting to Your Insurer

Notify your own insurance company promptly, even if you believe the other driver was at fault and even if you're not sure you'll file a claim. Policies generally require timely notice, and delaying can jeopardize coverage. Reporting is not the same as filing a claim; it opens a record and lets your insurer advise you. Have your photos, the other driver's information, and the police report number ready when you call.

How the Claim Unfolds

An adjuster will be assigned and will typically arrange an inspection or ask you to submit photos through an app. They'll determine fault based on the evidence, the police report, and both drivers' accounts, and will decide whether the car is repairable or a total loss. If it's declared a total loss, the payout is based on the vehicle's actual cash value, not what you owe on it — which is why a gap between the two can leave you still owing money on a car you no longer have. Keep every document, and get repair estimates in writing.

If the Other Driver Is Uninsured

This is where uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage earns its cost. If you carry it, your own insurer covers your injuries and, depending on your policy and state, property damage, then may pursue the other driver directly. Without it, your options narrow to your collision coverage if you have it, or pursuing the other driver personally, which is often impractical if they had no insurance to begin with. Verifying that you carry this coverage is a five-minute check worth doing before you ever need it.
